A 29-week preterm neonate, 1.1 kg, is grunting with deep chest retractions. The mother asks: "Why can't my baby just breathe like us?"

To answer, you need to know how breathing works.

The breathing pump

Muscles of inspiration

Core
  • Diaphragm (contracts, moves down)
  • External intercostals (lift the ribs)
Accessory
  • Sternocleidomastoid (lifts sternum)
  • Pectoralis minor (pulls ribs out)

Muscles of expiration

Usually passive
  • Diaphragm (relaxes, moves up)
  • The lung recoils like a balloon
When forced
  • Internal intercostals (pull ribs down)
  • Abdominals, quadratus lumborum
Why babies tireA newborn's ribs are soft and lie flat, and the diaphragm does almost all the work. When the lung is stiff (RDS), the soft chest wall caves in instead: those are the retractions you see.

Negative vs positive pressure

Same rule, opposite pushGas always flows from high to low pressure. The baby lowers the pressure inside; the ventilator raises it outside. Positive pressure can over-stretch the lung and squeeze the heart, so we use the lowest pressure that works.

Why we ventilate

Breathing has two jobs. When the baby can't do them, we help.

Two jobs: oxygen in, CO₂ out

Remember this split. When the blood gas shows high CO₂ (ABG: Basics), think minute ventilation. When oxygen is low, think FiO₂ and mean airway pressure.

Hypoxia, hypoxemia, ischemia

Three words that get mixed up. All mean tissues are short of oxygen, for different reasons.

Hypoxemia

Low O₂ in the blood.

  • Low FiO₂, hypoventilation
  • V/Q mismatch, diffusion problem
  • Right-to-left shunt (O₂ won't fix it)

Hypoxia

Low O₂ reaching the tissues.

  • Hypoxemia
  • Anaemia
  • Low cardiac output

Ischemia

Low blood flow to the tissues.

  • Poor arterial flow (shock)
  • Poor venous drainage

The ladder of support

"Ventilation's only purpose is to keep the patient alive while something good can happen to allow them to breathe for themselves."

Speaking ventilator

Nine words cover almost every ventilator setting.

Night round. The resident reads out the neonate's settings: "PIP 20, PEEP 5, Ti 0.35, rate 50, FiO₂ 40%."

By the end of this section, you will be able to picture that breath.

One breath, labelled

PIP

Peak pressure. Bigger PIP → bigger breath. Usually 16–20.

PEEP

Floor pressure. Stops collapse. 4 for normal lungs, 5–8 for sick lungs.

Ti / Te

Breath-in time (0.3–0.4 s) and breath-out time.

Rate

Machine breaths per minute.

FiO₂

Oxygen fraction: 21% (air) to 100%.

Tidal volume

Volume per breath. Aim 4–6 mL/kg.

Minute ventilation

Rate × tidal volume. Controls CO₂.

MAP

Mean airway pressure. Drives oxygenation.

Quick check

Pocket card: modes in one line each

  • IMV: machine breathes on its own clock, ignores the baby. Fights happen.
  • SIMV: set number of breaths, timed with the baby. Extra efforts: no help.
  • A/C: every effort gets a full breath. Set rate is only the back-up.
  • PSV: every effort helped, and the baby decides when to stop (flow-cycled).
  • Watch the baby: a rising rate on A/C or PSV can over-ventilate, so check CO₂.

Settings by disease

Different lungs, different problems, different starting settings.

Time constant: how fast the lung empties

τ = resistance × compliance. After 1 τ, 63% of the breath is out; after 3 τ, 95%. If Te is shorter than 3 τ, air stays behind.

Quick check

Pocket card: ventilation in six lines

  1. Breathing pulls air in (negative pressure). Ventilators push it in (positive pressure).
  2. A ventilator is a T-piece with an electronic thumb.
  3. PIP and rate move CO₂. FiO₂ and MAP move oxygen.
  4. Every breath: trigger (start), limit (max), cycling (end).
  5. Common modes: SIMV, A/C, PSV. They differ in what happens when the baby tries.
  6. Te ≥ 3 time constants, or air gets trapped.
