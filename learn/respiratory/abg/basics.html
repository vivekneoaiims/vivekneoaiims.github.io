Module 1

Why blood gas?

Blood is a seesaw. One side is an acid the lungs control, the other a base the kidneys control. Keep it level and the baby's cells work.

Baby Riya, 30 weeks, 4 hours old. Grunting, chest pulling in, on CPAP.

The resident says, “Let's send a gas.”

What can one blood sample tell us that the monitor can't? Let's find out.

Two players, two speeds

CO₂ is an acid. Breathe less and it piles up. HCO₃⁻ is a base. The kidneys hold on to it or let it go.

Tip the seesaw

Move the sliders, or tap a situation. Watch pH follow.

pH7.40Normal

mmHg, normal 35–45
mEq/L, normal 20–24

Level seesaw. This is where we want every baby.

Small number, big change

pH 7.1 looks close to 7.4. It isn't. pH is a log scale, so every 0.3 drop doubles the acid.

Why babies tip easily

Fewer buffers in blood, kidneys still immature, lungs fragile. Small insults swing pH fast.

Why we send a gas

It's the only test that shows acid, CO₂ and oxygen together. It's the gold standard for breathing and perfusion.

Quick check

Pocket card: the seesaw

  • CO₂ = acid, set by the lungs (minutes).
  • HCO₃⁻ = base, set by the kidneys (days).
  • pH below 7.35 = acidosis. Above 7.45 = alkalosis.
  • Every 0.3 fall in pH = double the acid.

Module 2

Getting a good sample

A perfect reading of a bad sample is still wrong. Most “strange” gases are made in the syringe, not in the baby.

10:00 am. A gas is drawn from Baby Riya. The machine is busy, so the syringe waits on the counter with a tiny bubble at the top.

10:45 am. Report: PO₂ 108. Her monitor showed SpO₂ 90% all morning.

Is Riya getting too much oxygen? Or is the syringe lying?

Where to sample from

A capillary sample mixes arterial and venous blood, so its PO₂ is meaningless. Use the pulse oximeter for oxygen instead (Module 5).

Truth in the baby

What the report says

    Delay follows the workbook table: every 10 minutes at 37 °C pH falls 0.01, pCO₂ rises 0.1 and PO₂ falls 0.1 mmHg; on ice (4 °C) it is ten times slower. Bubble and heparin sizes are illustrative.

    Back to Riya: a bubble plus 45 minutes of waiting explains that PO₂ of 108. Try it: tick the bubble and slide to 45 min. The air bubble is the main culprit.

    Six habits for a clean gas

    Why type in temperature, FiO₂ and Hb?The machine measures at 37 °C and calculates oxygen values from what you enter. A cooled baby or a wrong FiO₂ gives wrong numbers. See them on the printout in Module 3.

    Quick check

    Pocket card: clean gas checklist

    1. Arterial line is best. Heel is fine for pH and CO₂, never PO₂.
    2. No bubbles. Cap it. Roll gently.
    3. Analyse within 15 min, or within 1 hour on ice slush.
    4. Enter baby's temperature, FiO₂ and Hb.
    5. Weird result? Suspect the syringe before the baby.

    Module 3

    Reading the printout

    The machine prints 20-odd lines. It actually measures only a few. Know which numbers are real and which are maths.

    3 am. The gas machine beeps and prints a long slip. The intern reads it top to bottom and gets lost by line six.

    Which lines matter first? Tap through the slip below.

    Tap any line

    Start at the top

    Tap pH, then pCO₂, then HCO₃⁻. Those three are 90% of the story.

    The machine only truly measures three gas valuespH, pCO₂ and PO₂, plus electrolytes and lactate on many machines. HCO₃⁻, base excess and saturation are calculated from them. If the sample was bad (Module 2), every calculated line is bad too.

    Normal values for newborns

    From the Neonatal Ventilation Workbook 2026.

    Remember three round numberspH 7.40, pCO₂ 40, HCO₃⁻ 24. Every interpretation compares against these.

    Two habits matter as much as the numbers. Read the gas with the baby, not in isolation. And compare with the last gas: the trend tells you if your treatment is working.

    Quick check

    Pocket card: the printout

    • Read first: pH → pCO₂ → HCO₃⁻, then PO₂.
    • Measured: pH, pCO₂, PO₂ (plus Na, K, lactate).
    • Calculated: HCO₃⁻, BE, sO₂. Only as good as the sample.
    • Normal: pH 7.35–7.45, pCO₂ 35–45, PO₂ 50–70, HCO₃⁻ 20–24, BE −4 to +2. Hypercapnia: pCO₂ above 50.
    • Always compare with the previous gas.

    Module 4

    Four steps to any ABG

    Acid or alkali? Lungs or kidneys? Is the other side helping? One problem or two? That's the whole method.

    Riya's gas from the printout: pH 7.26, pCO₂ 55, HCO₃⁻ 24.0.

    By the end of this page you'll name it in ten seconds.

    The four questions

    Why “opposite” for lungs?

    CO₂ is acid. More CO₂ means a lower pH, so they move in opposite directions.

    Why “same” for metabolic?

    HCO₃⁻ is base. Less HCO₃⁻ means a lower pH, so they move together.

    In step 2, base excess backs up HCO₃⁻: below −4 points to metabolic acidosis, above +2 to metabolic alkalosis.

    Step 3 is compensation. The healthy organ pushes the other way to rescue pH:

    Uncompensated

    Other side still normal. pH off.

    Partially

    Other side moving. pH still off.

    Fully

    Other side moved. pH back in range.

    Step 4: one problem or two?Simple disorder: pCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ move the same direction. Mixed disorder: they move in opposite directions, both dragging pH the same way. Example: a baby with RDS (CO₂ ↑) who becomes septic (HCO₃⁻ ↓).

    The ABG decoder

    Type any gas or pick one. The decoder walks the four steps for you.

      Decoder uses the workbook's neonatal ranges: pH 7.35–7.45, pCO₂ 35–45, HCO₃⁻ 20–24. Is the compensation the right amount? That's Advanced: Winter's formula.

      Quick check

      Want more? The ABG sorter has 13 gases to race through.

      Pocket card: four steps

      1. pH: below 7.35 acidosis, above 7.45 alkalosis. Normal pH can still hide a disorder, so always do steps 2–4.
      2. Culprit: CO₂ moved opposite to pH → respiratory. HCO₃⁻ (and BE) moved same way → metabolic.
      3. Helper: pH normal = well compensated, close to normal = partial, far off = uncompensated.
      4. Simple or mixed: pCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ same direction = simple. Opposite = mixed. Call senior.

      Then: find and treat the cause.

      Module 5

      Oxygen: PO₂ vs SpO₂

      Two numbers, two different things. Mixing them up is how preterm babies get too much oxygen.

      Riya is on 40% oxygen. The monitor shows SpO₂ 99%. Everyone relaxes.

      Should they? What could her PO₂ be?

      The bus and the bus stop

      PO₂ comes from the blood gas. SpO₂ comes from the pulse oximeter. At 100% SpO₂ the PO₂ could be 80 or 300: the oximeter can't tell.

      Slide the oxygen

      Babies carry fetal haemoglobin, which grabs oxygen tighter. Same PO₂, higher SpO₂.

      Baby SpO₂ (fetal Hb)–
      Adult SpO₂–
      Verdict–

      Curves are a teaching model (P50 about 19 for fetal and 27 for adult Hb). Real curves shift with pH, temperature and transfusion.

      Too little hurts. Too much hurts too.

      Too little (PO₂ < 50)

      Brain and gut injury, acid build-up. Trials found that very low SpO₂ targets increased deaths.

      Too much (PO₂ > 70)

      Damages a preterm baby's eyes (ROP), lungs (BPD) and brain. Often hidden behind a “perfect” 99%.

      The bedside rulePreterm baby on oxygen with SpO₂ above the target range (commonly 90–95%)? Turn the oxygen down. Follow your unit's alarm limits.

      Back to Riya: 99% on 40% oxygen means her PO₂ could be anywhere above 80, so wean her FiO₂.

      Quick check

      Pocket card: oxygen

      • PO₂ = dissolved oxygen (gas). SpO₂ = Hb seats filled (oximeter).
      • Target PO₂ 50–70 mmHg in neonates.
      • Fetal Hb: high SpO₂ at low PO₂.
      • SpO₂ near 100% on oxygen can hide hyperoxia. Wean.
      • Heel-prick PO₂ is unreliable. Trust the oximeter.

      Module 6

      Practice: the ABG sorter

      Thirteen gases. Sort each into one of four boxes. Build a streak.

      Ward round, 8 am. Thirteen gases from the night are waiting.

      Use the four steps from Module 4. The arrows will save you.

      Sort the gas

      Pocket card: the four boxes

      • Respiratory acidosis: pH ↓ CO₂ ↑. Apnoea, RDS, blocked tube.
      • Respiratory alkalosis: pH ↑ CO₂ ↓. Over-ventilation.
      • Metabolic acidosis: pH ↓ HCO₃⁻ ↓. Poor perfusion, sepsis, hypoxia, hypothermia, anaemia.
      • Metabolic alkalosis: pH ↑ HCO₃⁻ ↑. Too much bicarbonate, diuretics, vomiting.
